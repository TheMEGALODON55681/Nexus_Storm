## Summary

Quantum computing is a rapidly evolving field of computing that leverages the principles of quantum mechanics to process information in fundamentally new ways. Unlike classical computers, which use bits as the smallest unit of information (representing either 0 or 1), quantum computers use quantum bits, or qubits, which can exist in a superposition of both 0 and 1 simultaneously [1][3]. This property, along with entanglement and interference, gives quantum computers the theoretical capacity to solve certain classes of problems exponentially faster than classical machines [2].

The field has progressed significantly since the early theoretical foundations laid by Richard Feynman and David Deutsch in the 1980s. Today, major technology companies including IBM, Google, Microsoft, and a growing number of startups are racing to build practical quantum computers, with applications ranging from cryptography and drug discovery to optimization and materials science [4][5].

## Theoretical Foundations

The foundations of quantum computing rest on three core quantum-mechanical phenomena: **superposition**, **entanglement**, and **interference** [1].

Superposition allows a qubit to be in a combined state of 0 and 1 at the same time, with associated probability amplitudes. When measured, the qubit collapses to one of the two definite states [2][3]. A register of *n* qubits can therefore represent 2^n states simultaneously — a property that underlies the potential speedup of quantum algorithms.

Entanglement is a uniquely quantum phenomenon in which two or more qubits become correlated such that the state of one cannot be described independently of the others, even when separated by large distances [4]. This property enables coordinated multi-qubit operations and is essential to many quantum algorithms.

Interference allows the amplitudes of different computational paths to combine constructively or destructively, amplifying correct results and cancelling out incorrect ones [5].

## Hardware Implementations

Several distinct physical platforms are being explored to realize quantum computers [6]:

- **Superconducting qubits** — Used by IBM and Google, these rely on tiny superconducting circuits cooled to near absolute zero. They are currently the most mature platform in terms of qubit count.
- **Trapped ions** — Used by IonQ and Quantinuum, this approach traps individual ions using electromagnetic fields and manipulates them with lasers. Trapped-ion qubits typically achieve higher fidelity but slower operation.
- **Photonic qubits** — Encode information in light particles, offering room-temperature operation and natural integration with fiber-optic communication.
- **Topological qubits** — Pursued by Microsoft, this approach aims to use exotic particles to create inherently error-resistant qubits, although it remains largely theoretical.

## Quantum Algorithms

A small but powerful collection of quantum algorithms has been developed that provide significant speedups over their classical counterparts [7]:

**Shor's Algorithm** can factor large integers in polynomial time, threatening the security of widely deployed cryptographic systems like RSA. This single result is one of the main motivators for the development of post-quantum cryptography [8].

**Grover's Algorithm** provides a quadratic speedup for unstructured search problems, with practical implications for database search, optimization, and cryptanalysis.

**Variational Quantum Eigensolvers (VQE)** and the **Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)** are hybrid algorithms designed for near-term, noisy quantum hardware. They show promise for chemistry, materials science, and combinatorial optimization [9].

## Current Limitations

Despite rapid progress, quantum computing faces several major challenges [10]:

1. **Decoherence** — Qubits are extremely fragile and lose their quantum state when disturbed by environmental noise, limiting how long computations can run.
2. **Error rates** — Current qubits have non-trivial error rates, making large computations unreliable without sophisticated error-correction codes that require many physical qubits per logical qubit.
3. **Scalability** — While prototype machines now have hundreds to thousands of qubits, building error-corrected, fault-tolerant machines may require millions of physical qubits.
4. **Algorithm scarcity** — Beyond a handful of breakthrough algorithms, the catalogue of problems where quantum computers offer practical advantages is still narrow.

## Applications

Quantum computing is expected to have transformative impact in several domains [11][12]:

- **Cryptography & cybersecurity** — Both threatening current encryption and enabling new quantum-resistant protocols
- **Drug discovery** — Simulating molecular interactions at quantum accuracy to accelerate pharmaceutical research
- **Materials science** — Designing new materials with tailored properties for batteries, superconductors, and catalysts
- **Optimization** — Solving large-scale logistics, scheduling, and financial portfolio problems
- **Machine learning** — Exploring quantum-enhanced learning algorithms that may train faster or generalize better

## Outlook

The field is currently described as being in the **Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ)** era, where machines have enough qubits to perform interesting experiments but are still too noisy for the most demanding applications [13]. Demonstrations of *quantum advantage* — solving a specific problem faster than any classical computer — have been claimed for narrow tasks by Google and others, although these claims remain debated and the practical relevance of those tasks is limited.

The next major milestone is **fault-tolerant quantum computing**: machines capable of running long, error-corrected computations on logical qubits. Most experts expect this to arrive incrementally over the coming decade, with continued rapid hardware progress, deeper algorithmic theory, and growing investment from both governments and industry [14].
